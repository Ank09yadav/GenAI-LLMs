## 20. What are subword tokenization methods?

### Answer

**Subword tokenization methods** are data-driven algorithms that segment text into variable-length substrings. Frequently occurring words remain intact as single tokens (e.g., `"the"`, `"learning"`), while rare, compound, or misspelled words are decomposed into familiar subword morphemes (e.g., `"hyper"` + `"parameter"`, `"un"` + `"precedent"` + `"ed"`).

This ensures **high semantic compression and short sequence lengths for common vocabulary** while providing **zero Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV) risk** for novel, compound, or domain-specific terms.

```text
                               The Subword Segmentation Principle
                               
Frequent Words (High Compression):
"technology"        ──► ["technology"]              (1 token - dense semantics)

Compound / Complex Words (Morpheme Breakdown):
"microservices"     ──► ["micro", "services"]       (2 tokens - semantic decomposition)

Rare Words / Typos (Graceful Subword Fallback):
"tehcnology"        ──► ["te", "hc", "nology"]     (3 tokens - zero OOV failure!)

Non-Latin Characters / Emojis (Byte Fallback):
"🚀"                ──► [0xF0, 0x9F, 0x9A, 0x80]    (Mapped to UTF-8 bytes)
```

### In-Depth Mathematical Mechanics of Each Method

#### 1. Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE - Sennrich et al., 2016)
- **Philosophy:** Bottom-up deterministic frequency merging.
- **Algorithm:**
  1. Initialize the vocabulary with all base characters (or all 256 individual UTF-8 bytes in Byte-Level BPE).
  2. Tokenize the entire training corpus into individual symbols.
  3. Count the co-occurrence frequencies of all adjacent symbol pairs across the corpus.
  4. Select the single pair $(u, v)$ with the maximum frequency:
     $$(u^*, v^*) = \arg\max_{(u, v)} \operatorname{Count}(u, v)$$
  5. Merge all occurrences of $(u^*, v^*)$ into a new single vocabulary symbol $uv$.
  6. Repeat steps 3–5 iteratively until reaching the target vocabulary size $|V|$ (e.g., 32,000 or 128,256 merges).
- **Whitespace Handling:** Uses special characters like `Ġ` (in GPT tokenizers) or `_` to denote spaces prepended to words, preserving whitespace formatting.

---

#### 2. WordPiece (Schuster & Nakajima, 2012 / Devlin et al., 2018)
- **Philosophy:** Bottom-up probabilistic likelihood maximization.
- **Algorithm:**
  1. Similar to BPE, starts with base characters and iteratively merges pairs.
  2. **The Key Difference:** Instead of selecting the pair with the highest raw frequency, WordPiece selects the pair that **maximizes the likelihood of the training data** under a unigram language model.
  3. The selection score evaluates the mutual information between subwords $u$ and $v$:
     $$\operatorname{Score}(u, v) = \frac{\operatorname{Count}(uv)}{\operatorname{Count}(u) \times \operatorname{Count}(v)}$$
  4. Favors pairs whose components appear together significantly more often than would be predicted by chance.
- **Subword Marker:** Prefixes internal/continuation subwords with `##` (e.g., `"playing"` $\to$ `["play", "##ing"]`).

---

#### 3. Unigram Language Model (Kudo, 2018 / SentencePiece)
- **Philosophy:** Top-down probabilistic loss-based pruning.
- **Algorithm:**
  1. Initializes with an **oversized seed vocabulary** (e.g., 500,000 potential words, morphemes, and n-grams extracted from the corpus).
  2. Fits a unigram language model to compute the probability of every token $x$ in vocabulary $V$:
     $$P(x) = \frac{\operatorname{Count}(x)}{\sum_{w \in V} \operatorname{Count}(w)}$$
  3. Evaluates how much the overall corpus log-likelihood would decrease if token $x$ were completely removed from the vocabulary:
     $$\Delta \mathcal{L}(x) = \mathcal{L}(V) - \mathcal{L}(V \setminus \{x\})$$
  4. Prunes the bottom $10\% - 20\%$ of tokens that cause the smallest drop in corpus likelihood.
  5. Repeats iteratively until the vocabulary shrinks to the desired size (e.g., exactly 32,000 tokens).
- **Advantage:** Because it is probabilistic, Unigram tokenization can output multiple valid token segmentations with associated probabilities, enabling **Subword Regularization** (sampling different segmentations during training to improve model robustness).

### Key Point

> **Subword tokenization breaks rare words into frequent semantic morphemes; BPE merges frequent pairs, WordPiece merges high-mutual-information pairs, and Unigram prunes low-likelihood candidates from an oversized seed vocabulary.**

---

### Interview Answer

> **Subword tokenization bridges the gap between word-level and character-level models by keeping common words as single tokens while decomposing rare or unseen words into shared subword morphemes.
> 
> The three primary methods are:
> 1. **Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE):** A bottom-up deterministic algorithm that iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent symbol or byte pairs until reaching a target vocabulary size. It is the dominant choice for modern generative LLMs, including GPT-4 and LLaMA-3.
> 2. **WordPiece:** Similar to BPE, but merges pairs based on a likelihood ratio score ($\frac{\text{Count}(AB)}{\text{Count}(A)\text{Count}(B)}$), prioritizing pairs that appear together significantly more often than expected by chance (used in BERT).
> 3. **Unigram Language Model:** A top-down probabilistic method that starts with an oversized vocabulary and iteratively prunes candidates whose removal minimally impacts the corpus log-likelihood (used in SentencePiece and T5).**
